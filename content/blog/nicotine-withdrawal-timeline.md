The honest, complete timeline of nicotine withdrawal — what's happening physiologically, what you'll likely feel, and when each symptom typically resolves. Use it as a map; nothing about quitting is more demoralizing than thinking a symptom is permanent when it's actually scheduled to fade next week.

## Hour 0-12: clearing

**Physiologically**: nicotine half-life is two hours, so by hour 8 you've cleared about 75% of your last dose. Carbon monoxide drops by half. Blood oxygen normalizes.

**You'll feel**: heart rate calming, slightly cooler hands and feet (vasoconstriction relaxing), mild restlessness toward the end. First few cravings — short, manageable.

**Lasts**: this phase is just one cycle. You move into the next phase whether you do anything or not.

## Hour 12-72: peak acute withdrawal

This is the hardest window. By 72 hours, nicotine is essentially gone from your bloodstream, but your brain has been producing extra nicotinic receptors for years. They're still there, still firing, still asking for input that isn't coming.

**You'll feel**:

- **Cravings**: 3-5 minutes each, every 20-60 minutes when awake. Peaks at hour 48-72.
- **Irritability**: noticeable to other people. Snapping at small things.
- **Anxiety**: free-floating, not tied to anything specific.
- **Difficulty concentrating**: complex thought feels effortful.
- **Increased appetite**, especially for sugar.
- **Disrupted sleep**, vivid dreams.
- **Headache**, mild nausea, restlessness.

**Lasts**: 24-72 hours from your last cigarette, with the peak almost always between hour 48 and hour 72. Day 3 is the worst single day for most quitters.

We've written about [day 3 specifically](/blog/day-3-quitting-smoking) and [why day 3 is harder than day 1](/blog/day-1-vs-day-3-quitting-smoking) if you want more on this stage.

## Day 4-7: physical taper

The chemistry is largely past. Your brain is starting to downregulate the extra receptors it had been growing — a process that will continue for weeks but begins immediately.

**You'll feel**:

- **Cravings** still arrive but space out — minutes between, not seconds. Each one is shorter and less urgent.
- **Mood** still up and down, but with longer "normal" stretches between dips.
- **Energy** starting to come back. Some people feel measurably better; others still feel sluggish for another week.
- **Cough increases** as cilia in the lungs start clearing built-up debris. This is a sign of recovery, not relapse — it usually peaks around days 5-10 and resolves over the following month.
- **Taste and smell** begin to sharpen.

**Lasts**: through about day 7.

## Week 2-4: psychological dominant

Physical withdrawal is essentially complete by the end of week 2. The fight from here is *psychological* — habit, trigger, context — not chemistry.

**You'll feel**:

- **Situational cravings**: after meals, with alcohol, with coffee, during stress, in places where you used to smoke. These can be intense but are short and predictable.
- **Mood dips** that feel personal but aren't — your dopamine baseline is still recalibrating.
- **Boredom** in a way that's hard to describe. Smoking filled small dead moments; you'll notice those dead moments before you find new things to fill them.
- **Sleep** mostly normalizes by the end of week 3.
- **Cough** subsides.

**Lasts**: through about day 28.

This is the stage where most people who slip do so. The peak chemistry is behind you, which makes you feel like you've "won" and should be safe — and then a stressful day catches you. **Most slip-induced relapses happen in week 2-4, not week 1.** Plan accordingly.

## Month 1-3: rewiring

Your brain is largely done growing back to its non-smoker shape.

**You'll feel**:

- **Cravings** are rare and almost always trigger-driven. Days or even weeks go by without one.
- **Vivid dreams** sometimes persist, particularly if you were on the patch. Usually harmless.
- **Sense of smell and taste** are noticeably sharper.
- **Lung function** has measurably improved (FEV1 up roughly 10%).
- **Stamina** noticeably better. Walking up stairs is easier.

**Lasts**: through about day 90.

## Month 3+: maintenance

You're a non-smoker with a history. Cravings still happen occasionally — at funerals, weddings, particular high-stress moments — but they're rare and easy to ride out.

**You'll feel**:

- **Cravings** occasional, brief, situational. Manageable with a 5-minute distraction.
- **Health markers** continue to improve for years. At 1 year, heart-disease risk is cut roughly in half from baseline; at 5 years, stroke risk approaches that of a never-smoker; at 10 years, lung-cancer risk is roughly half of a continuing smoker's.

## What "post-acute withdrawal" actually means

You may read about PAWS — *post-acute withdrawal syndrome.* It's not officially recognized in the way alcohol PAWS is, but it's a real phenomenon people describe: occasional bouts of low mood, restlessness, or sleep disruption that come and go for months. If you experience this, you're not doing it wrong. It tends to fade gradually over the first year.

## The summary you can save

- **20 min**: heart rate normalizes
- **8 hr**: CO halves
- **24-72 hr**: peak acute withdrawal (day 3 is the bottom)
- **Day 7**: acute physical symptoms mostly done
- **Week 2-4**: psychological withdrawal dominant; **most slips happen here**
- **Month 1**: senses sharper, lung function measurably up
- **Month 3**: largely rewired; you're a non-smoker
- **Year 1**: heart disease risk halved
- **Year 5**: stroke risk approaches never-smoker baseline
- **Year 10**: lung cancer risk roughly half of a continuing smoker's
