Most people quit smoking without ever telling their doctor. That's a missed opportunity — not because doctors have a magic word, but because **the success rates for quit attempts with clinical support are roughly double the unaided rate**, and a 10-minute conversation gets you that support.

Here's what to ask for, what to bring, and what to say if your doctor isn't being especially helpful.

## What to actually ask for

There are three categories of cessation help your doctor can prescribe or recommend:

### 1. Nicotine Replacement Therapy (NRT)

The most common and the most established. Several forms:

- **Patches** (21mg, 14mg, 7mg) — slow, steady release. Usually a 6-8 week taper.
- **Gum** (2mg, 4mg) — fast-acting, use as needed for cravings.
- **Lozenges** — similar to gum, less jaw work.
- **Inhaler** or **nasal spray** — fastest-acting forms, prescription only.

Patches and gum are over-the-counter in most countries. But getting your doctor to *prescribe* them often means insurance covers them — which can be the difference between $50/month out of pocket and $0.

**Ask for**: a prescription for whichever form you want, even if it's OTC, so insurance pays.

### 2. Varenicline (Chantix)

A prescription pill, taken twice daily. Reduces craving and blunts the reward from any cigarette you do smoke. Highest success rate of any single intervention in cessation trials — roughly 2x patches alone.

Side effects: vivid dreams (very common, usually harmless), nausea in the first week, occasionally low mood. Worth knowing about; not worth dismissing the drug over.

**Ask for**: "Is varenicline a fit for me? What would the prescribing look like?"

### 3. Bupropion (Zyban)

An antidepressant repurposed for cessation. Less effective than varenicline but a real option, especially for people who also have low mood. Twice-daily pill.

**Ask for**: "Would bupropion make sense, particularly given [any mood symptoms]?"

You can combine NRT with either oral medication — that's standard practice.

## What to bring

The single most useful thing you can bring is a one-page summary of your actual smoking pattern. Most doctors will ask in vague terms ("how much do you smoke?") and you'll answer in vague terms back, and a real conversation doesn't happen.

Show up with:

- **How many cigarettes per day** (a real number, not "a pack-ish").
- **What time you have your first cigarette in the morning.** This is the single best clinical predictor of nicotine dependence — first-cigarette-within-30-minutes-of-waking signals heavy dependence and is usually how doctors decide on dose.
- **Any previous quit attempts**: when, how long they lasted, what ended them.
- **Any medications or conditions** that might interact (especially for bupropion).
- **Your craving patterns**, if you've been tracking them. "Most cravings hit between 3-6pm after work" gives the doctor something concrete to work with.

## How to bring it up

The most common reason this conversation doesn't happen is that nobody starts it. Doctors are trained to ask about smoking, but in a busy 12-minute visit, the question gets reduced to a yes/no and that's it.

The line that works: **"I want to quit smoking and I want your help with the medical side. Can we spend a few minutes on that today, or should I schedule a follow-up specifically for it?"**

That sentence:

- Names the goal explicitly.
- Asks for clinical, not motivational, support — which is what doctors are actually trained to provide.
- Acknowledges the time constraint and offers a path.

A 10-minute follow-up visit specifically for cessation will get you more than a 30-second mention at the end of an annual physical.

## When your doctor isn't being especially helpful

Some don't know much about cessation medications. Some give the wave-off ("you just need to want it more"). Some prescribe one round of patches and call it a day.

If that happens:

- **Ask for a referral to a tobacco-treatment specialist.** Many large hospital systems and most state quitlines have one. The visit is usually covered.
- **Call 1-800-QUIT-NOW** (US). Free, all 50 states, staffed by counselors who can prescribe in many states or coordinate with your doctor.
- **For varenicline specifically**: telehealth services like Done, Hims, and several others prescribe it after a brief consult. Costs roughly $40-80/month out of pocket if insurance doesn't cover.

If your doctor told you to "just use willpower," that is not medical advice — that is the absence of medical advice. You can do better.

## What you don't need to do

- **You don't need to have already quit** before you ask. The conversation is more useful *before* your quit date than after.
- **You don't need to justify the request.** "I want to quit and I want help" is enough.
- **You don't need to apologize for asking about medication.** Cessation drugs are routine prescriptions, not a sensitive request.

## TL;DR

- Ask specifically for **NRT (prescribed, so insurance covers), varenicline, or bupropion** — usually some combination.
- Bring **cigs/day, time of first cigarette, prior attempts, and any tracker data** to the visit.
- Open with: "I want to quit smoking and I want your help with the medical side."
- If you don't get useful help, **call 1-800-QUIT-NOW** or ask for a tobacco-treatment specialist referral.
